Across the reviewed files, anxiety consistently emerges as the primary limiting factor for performance and embodiment in wearable robots because it alters sensory precision, belief updating about safety, and interpretation of bodily feedback under uncertainty, rather than reflecting general cognitive limitation ([[Target word 'anxiety or threat or safety or risk and (somatosensory_cortex or gyrus or frontal lobe or M1 or M2 or norepinephrine or adrenaline or amygdala or bio or insula) and (embodiment or ownership or agency or proprioception or trust)']]). 
Findings show that increased state or trait anxiety predicts higher perceived workload and effort despite unchanged objective task performance ([[Human–Robot Interactions A Pilot Study of Psychoaffective and Cognitive Factors to Boost the Acceptance and Usability of Assistive Wearable Devices#Key Findings]]). This dissociation indicates that anxiety does not reliably degrade sensorimotor accuracy or cognitive capacity itself, but instead biases subjective evaluation of bodily control, effort, and safety ([[Anxiety and Proprioception#^a442f9]]). 
In addition, [[Target word 'cognitive or perception']] shows that anxiety primarily alters _perceptual inference_ rather than sensory accuracy or cognitive capacity: placebo/nocebo manipulations of anxiety changed how participants _felt_ their proprioception and bodily stability without affecting objective joint position accuracy, while higher anxiety reliably predicted greater sway, delayed sensory reweighting, and altered reliance on visual and somatosensory input. This narrows the mechanism further by locating anxiety’s effect at the perception–embodiment interface, where bodily signals are interpreted, trusted, and integrated into the body model, rather than at the level of sensory encoding, motor execution, or general cognition.

In wearable robotics, where external forces perturb the body, this bias leads users to interpret robotic feedback as potentially unsafe or unreliable, thereby weakening embodiment even when control performance is preserved ([[Embodiment for Robotic Lower-LimbExoskeletons A Narrative Review#1. **Embodiment Drives Functional Success**]]).
The files further demonstrate that anxiety shifts motor behavior toward rigid or unstable strategies by disrupting multisensory integration and delaying sensory reweighting under perturbation, effects that occur independently of cognitive reserve ([[Anxiety and Proprioception#^t2]]; [[Target word 'cognitive or perception']]). Theoretical and empirical work within the corpus shows that anxiety biases predictive processing toward punishment cues, amplifying threat-related signals and reducing confidence in sensory feedback rather than improving error correction ([[A predictive processing perspective of disrupted motor control under anxiety]]). In wearable robots, this results in reduced acceptance of artificial proprioceptive signals and slower integration of the device into the body model ([[Proprioception and Sensory Dynamics in Wearable Robotics#^trust-embodiment]]). 
Familiarization reliably reduces anxiety while improving perceived safety and usability, reinforcing the interpretation that anxiety precedes and constrains embodiment rather than emerging as a consequence of poor embodiment ([[Human–Robot Interactions A Pilot Study of Psychoaffective and Cognitive Factors to Boost the Acceptance and Usability of Assistive Wearable Devices#^c1d74f]]).
