## Brain Mechanisms Regarding Embodiment
- Feedforward theory of movement control:
> Every time a motor program 
(**afferent signal**) is sent from the primary motor cortex (M1) to the motor neurons in the spinal cord,  copy of the descending signal is generated in parallel (i.e., **efference copy**), allowing the nervous system to predict the sensory consequences of the programmed movement.

---
**Sensory attenuation** = How well the efference and afferent signals match

In other words, how well the expected and actual movements match.

> This suggests that wearing an exoskeleton could lead to a sense of disembodiment, even without movement. In fact, simply wearing an exoskeleton can alter proprioceptive feedback. This is in line with previous research showing that wearing exoskeletons or prosthetics can influence proprioception and body schema, with the experience of either embodiment or disembodiment depending on how the wearer interacts with the device20. In this context, it is crucial to emphasize that factors such as the weight of the exoskeleton are not confounding variables, but rather a central component of the sensorimotor experience under investigation. Prior studies have shown that changes in sensory feedback resulting from physical properties like device weight can alter sensorimotor integration, thereby influencing the sense of embodiment20,22. Therefore, the increased N60 amplitude may partly reflect the nervous system’s response to disrupted proprioceptive predictions induced by the exoskeleton’s physical characteristics. Regarding the mixed-stimulation condition, no differences were observed compared to the self-stimulation condition. The purpose of introducing this intermediate condition was to experimentally modulate the user’s perceived control over the action by passively moving the user’s leg through external activation of the exoskeleton. However, due to the characteristics of the actuators, our exoskeleton was unable to move the subjects passively without their active involvement. The ALICE exoskeleton is an assistive device, meaning its actuators do not fully support the movement of a healthy adult leg. As a result, the user’s active participation is necessary to complete the movement, making this condition effectively comparable to the self-stimulation condition. EEG data align with participants’ self-reported ratings of perceived stimulation intensity. At the behavioral level, participants rated the perceived intensity as highest in the external stimulation condition, intermediate in the mixed stimulation condition, and lowest in the self-stimulation condition, despite the stimulation intensity being the same across all conditions. This effect, widely documented in the literature, seems primarily mediated by attentional processes23,24. Notably, modulation of subjective stimulus intensity was significant only in the exoskeleton conditions. This effect may reflect a state of disembodiment: the greater mismatch between predicted and actual sensory input in these conditions likely increases demands on attentional resources. Supporting this, a qualitative study found that simply wearing a heavy exoskeleton makes users constantly aware of the device, heightening their overall sensory awareness25. By contrast, in non- exoskeleton conditions, more accurate sensorimotor predictions reduce the need for such attentional shifts, leading to diminished modulation of perceived stimulus intensity. In our study, this is further supported by the strong negative correlation between self-reported intensity scores and the amplitude of the central N100. Research on attention orienting in the somatosensory modality has shown that the N100 is a marker of attention orientation to external somatosensory stimuli, which mediates the perception of stimulus intensity24. In this study, the N100 was significantly more negative in the external stimulation conditions compared to self- stimulation, regardless of the presence of the exoskeleton. These results likely reflect the natural tendency to focus on external stimuli, which are perceived as more evolutionarily significant than self-generated stimuli and, as a result, are subjectively experienced as more intense.
