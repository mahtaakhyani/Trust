Biological research suggests that **low-frequency noise (LFN)** can trigger physiological stress responses—including cortisol release.

- **Subcortical Processing:** Low-frequency sounds (typically between **20 Hz and 200 Hz**) can be processed by the brain's subcortical regions (like the amygdala) before the conscious "thinking" part of your brain (the cerebral cortex) even registers them.
- **The Infrasound Effect:** If the frequency is very low (Infrasound, <20 Hz), you might not "hear" it at all, but your inner ear (the vestibular system) and your skin can still detect the vibrations.
- **Cortisol Release:** Studies have shown that prolonged exposure to LFN can lead to an increase in **cortisol** and **adrenaline**, as the body treats the persistent vibration as a potential environmental threat, regardless of your mood.

### Physical Response
**The Vagus Nerve:** Physical vibrations can stimulate the vagus nerve or the endocrine system directly, prompting a hormonal shift even in people who describe themselves as "not sensitive" to noise.

This is the most "dangerous" part of LFN. Because you don't feel "anxious," you don't remove yourself from the sound.

- **Phase 1 (Seconds):** Subconscious alertness (Nervous system).
- **Phase 2 (Minutes):** Onset of cortisol production (Hormonal system).
- **Phase 3 (Hours):** Physiological fatigue. You might feel drained, have a headache, or feel irritable, but you won't know why.

### Randomized 
If a low-frequency sound is **constant** (like the hum of a distant plane), your brain eventually labels it as "background" and tries to stop the cortisol production.

When it is **random**, the brain never gets that "all clear" signal. Every time the sound happens, it restarts the instant neurological alarm, keeping your cortisol production "turned on" indefinitely. This is exactly why it destroys trust—you are essentially living in a state of "permanent surprise."
