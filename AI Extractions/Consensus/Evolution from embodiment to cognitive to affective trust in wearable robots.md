
Wearable robots often start as external tools but can become integrated into the user's sensorimotor system through multi-modal sensory feedback, neuromuscular interfaces, and biomechatronic control, which enhance the user's perception of the device as part of their own body ([[#^85f20f|Xia et al., 2024]]; [[#^e2addf|Dowdall et al., 2025]]). 

This embodiment process involves not only improved motor control but also the development of a sensory representation of the artificial limb, which correlates with increased subjective feelings of ownership and agency over the device ([[#^e2addf|Dowdall et al., 2025]]; [[#^45523c|Lee & Huang, 2025]]). 

Cognitive trust—based on the robot’s reliability and functionality—can evolve into affective trust as the user experiences a more natural, embodied interaction, supported by continuous sensory feedback and adaptive control ([[#^1a0db0|Nassour et al., 2024]]; [[#^7aa660|Chen et al., 2018]]). ^cogtrust

Additionally, wearable affective robots that integrate brain-computer interfaces and emotion recognition aim to deepen this affective connection by understanding and responding to the user's emotional states, further promoting embodiment and trust ([[#^7aa660|Chen et al., 2018]]). 
Thus, the transition from cognitive to affective trust aligns closely with the technological embodiment process, where the robot becomes not just a tool but an extension of the self.

## Timeline
- **Initial period (first contact)** – Users judge the robot’s _purpose_ and _process_ before any performance is observed. The stated motives/intentions and how clearly the system’s operation is explained (understandability, familiarity) dominate early trust [(Stirling et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07200).[[Measuring Trust for Exoskeleton Systems]]
- Immediate “task‑intervention” or “delegation” cues (e.g., does the user let the exoskeleton start a step?) are the first observable behaviors that confirm or reject these expectations [(Stirling et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07200).

- **Mid‑interaction (after a few cycles of use)** – Direct experience of **_performance_** becomes central. Perceived **predictability** and **supportiveness** drop sharply when error rates rise, showing that users rely on the robot’s consistency and ability to meet task goals [(Stirling et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07200). At this stage **_reliability_** and **_dependability_** emerge as the strongest predictors of trust accumulation, while any violations trigger rapid trust loss [(Alhaji et al., 2021)](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2021.703504). Behavioral‑change measures (e.g., altered gait or muscle activation) provide the surrogate data that the researcher can use to track this shift [(Stirling et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07200). #trust_factors/robot_factors/reliability #trust_factors/robot_factors/dependability #trust_factors/robot_factors/performance #trust_factors/robot_factors/predictability #trust_factors/robot_factors/supportiveness 
- **Final period (limited‑time end point)** – Trust stabilizes into a composite rating that reflects overall **confidence**, **faith**, and **perceived autonomy**. Users who have seen the robot succeed repeatedly show higher overall trust, while those who experienced failures retain lower scores despite any apologies or “repair” expressions  [(Alhaji et al., 2021)](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2021.703504). The timing of trust‑rating itself is driven mainly by **task progress** rather than moment‑to‑moment performance, indicating that once the interaction ends, the user’s mental model of the robot is the decisive factor [(Dekarske et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00238).
