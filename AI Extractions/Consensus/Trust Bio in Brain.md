Trust and distrust engage distinct but overlapping brain regions, reflecting their different psychological processes. Trust is associated with activation in the caudate nucleus and anterior paracingulate cortex, which are linked to positive trust decisions, while distrust correlates more with activity in the prefrontal cortex (especially ventromedial and orbitofrontal areas), insula, and amygdala, regions involved in negative evaluations and risk assessment (Eskander et al., 2020; Dimoka, 2010). 

The temporoparietal junction (TPJ), precuneus, inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), and medial prefrontal cortex are involved when individuals explicitly control or adjust their attitudes of trust and distrust, with the precuneus linked to trust control and IFG to distrust control (Filkowski et al., 2016). 

Electroencephalogram studies show that frontal lobes are active during trust (alpha and beta waves), whereas temporal lobes show activity during distrust (gamma waves) (Oh et al., 2022). Oxytocin modulates trust by reducing amygdala activation and related fear processing regions, facilitating sustained trust even after breaches, highlighting the amygdala’s role in distrust or fear of betrayal (Baumgartner et al., 2008). 

Additionally, individual differences in trust propensity relate to gray matter volume and connectivity across networks involved in cost-benefit calculations and social inference, indicating a complex neural basis for varying trust behaviors (Feng et al., 2020).

Overall, key brain areas correlated with trust include the caudate nucleus, anterior paracingulate cortex, TPJ, precuneus, and medial prefrontal cortex; distrust involves the amygdala, insula, orbitofrontal cortex, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and inferior frontal gyrus (Eskander et al., 2020; Dimoka, 2010; Filkowski et al., 2016; Oh et al., 2022; Baumgartner et al., 2008).
