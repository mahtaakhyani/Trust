Cognitive beliefs about safety are updated because initial beliefs are often biased or incomplete, and [[new]] information—especially threatening or disconfirming evidence—can prompt revision to better reflect reality. ^tr-gya2bn50t

Humans tend to integrate desirable (positive) information more readily than undesirable (negative) information, creating an optimism bias that supports well-being but can underestimate risks (Garrett et al., 2018). 
However, **this bias diminishes under perceived threat**, leading to more balanced belief updating that incorporates negative information to enhance caution and safety (Garrett et al., 2018; Berón et al., 2024). ^bias-update

Updating beliefs is also influenced by anxiety levels and environmental uncertainty
^upda

for example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, people showed more rational belief updating compared to pre-pandemic times when optimism bias was stronger (Khalid et al., 2025; Berón et al., 2024).  ^9d5d61

Some individuals, such as those with PTSD symptoms, may resist updating safety-related beliefs even when presented with disconfirming evidence, which can maintain maladaptive threat perceptions (Haim-Nachum et al., 2024). 

Cognitive-behavioral interventions work by inducing uncertainty in rigid beliefs rather than simply replacing initial beliefs, facilitating flexible updating that supports adaptive behavior and resilience (Smith et al., 2020). Thus, belief updating is necessary because initial cognitive beliefs are not fixed truths but hypotheses continuously refined by [[new]] evidence and context.
