## **How Cognitive Processes Shape Perceptions of Safety and “No Harm”** ^tr-p8l2ty16l

Perceiving something as safe (not harmful) is largely a **cognitive construction**: the brain interprets information about threats, our own abilities, and the context, then tags a situation as “safe enough” or “unsafe.” ^cog

## Core Cognitive Steps in Perceiving Safety

1. **Detecting and Interpreting Risk Cues**
    
    - People scan the environment for hazard signals and judge probability and severity of harm (e.g., miners judging dangerous scenarios, drivers detecting traffic hazards) (Shi et al., 2021; Moran et al., 2020).
    - This involves attention, perception, and interpretation of hazard information (Shi et al., 2021; Tashjian et al., 2020).
2. **Using Heuristics and Biases**
    
    - When information is uncertain or complex, the brain simplifies with heuristics (rules of thumb), which can distort safety judgments.
    - **Anchoring:** initial “risk anchors” (past experiences, given information) strongly bias whether behavior is felt safe or risky (Qiu et al., 2024).
    - Overconfidence, optimism, and illusion of control can make unsafe situations _feel_ safe (Purushothaman et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2024).
3. **Integrating Knowledge, Experience, and Beliefs**
    
    - Safety knowledge, prior accidents, and personal experience shape what is seen as harmful or safe (De Freitas et al., 2019; Qiu et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2021).
    - Social and organizational signals (safety climate, leadership, norms, trust) calibrate cognitive risk perception: strong safety climate lowers perceived risk; high workload or prior accidents increase it (Oah et al., 2018; Rundmo, 2000; Taylor & Snyder, 2017; Cai et al., 2023; Jang et al., 2020).
4. **Self- and Threat-Oriented Evaluation**
    
    - Safety is computed not just as “low threat” but also as “I can cope/control this” (self-efficacy, control) (Tashjian et al., 2020; Cai et al., 2023).
    - Cognitive models distinguish **threat-oriented evaluations** (how bad/how likely) from **self-oriented evaluations** (can I handle it?), whose interaction produces a sense of safeness (Tashjian et al., 2020; Gilbert, 2024).

## Safety Learning and Updating

- Cognitive instructions (reappraisal, CBT-like explanations) can help update “this is dangerous” to “this is now safe,” but people high in anxiety/uncertainty struggle more to make this update (Wake et al., 2020).
- Trust (in government, technology, teams) acts as a cognitive shortcut that lowers perceived risk, sometimes appropriately, sometimes leading to underestimation of danger (Jang et al., 2020; Kimpe et al., 2021; Pearson, 2019). ^shortcut

## Conclusion

Perception of “no harm” is not a direct readout of the world; it is a multi-step cognitive process that interprets risk cues, uses shortcuts and biases, blends in knowledge, experience, and social context, and evaluates both threat and one’s own coping ability to label a person, object, or situation as safe or unsafe.

## Cognitive trust

Cognitive trust often shapes the perception of physical safety, but also evaluations continue after physical safety is experienced. 
In studies on automation use, **trust as a cognitive process comes before perceived safety** (Nordhoff & Hagenzieker, 2024).  ^cog1

Workplace safety research highlights that mental health and cognitive factors are integral to both perceiving and maintaining physical safety, implying **ongoing cognitive appraisal even after physical safety is established** (Tedder et al., 2025).

Safety-specific trust includes both cognition-based (rational evaluation) and affect-based (emotional response) components, with **cognition-based trust helping to mitigate stressors that impact safety behaviors** (Wang et al., 2020). 

In relationships, cognitive biases can influence how individuals interpret physical safety experiences, showing that cognition continuously shapes trust even after physical events occur (Formo, 2017). 

Overall, cognitive processes not only initiate trust but also adapt and respond following experiences of physical safety or harm, indicating a reciprocal relationship rather than a strict sequence (Nordhoff & Hagenzieker, 2024; Wang et al., 2020; Formo, 2017). ^ov
